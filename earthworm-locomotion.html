Lumbricus terrestris  /  locomotion

An earthworm has
no legs, no bones,
and two muscles.

It gets around by changing shape — long and thin, then short and fat — and holding on to the soil with bristles while it does. Here's the anatomy, then the cycle, one step at a time.

      Now: Anchor the rear Circular Longitudinal

        There are no bones to push against — so the worm pushes against its own water.

        Each segment is a sealed bag of fluid. Fluid can't be squashed smaller, so when the circular muscles squeeze inwards, the fluid has nowhere to go but lengthways — the segment gets longer and narrower. When the longitudinal muscles pull the ends together, the same fluid bulges outwards and the segment gets shorter and fatter. Biologists call this a hydrostatic skeleton.

        The two muscle layers are antagonistic: neither can stretch itself back out, so each one resets the other. Circular contracts, longitudinal is stretched. Longitudinal contracts, circular is stretched. That's the whole engine.

        The setae are what stop the worm sliding backwards. Without them it would stretch and shrink on the spot and go nowhere — which is exactly what happens to a worm on glass.
